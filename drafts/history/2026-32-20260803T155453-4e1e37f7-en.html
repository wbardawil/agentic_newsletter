The market is repricing AI narratives. Boards next.

Insight summary: Boards are demanding AI productivity gains that executives describe as 95% future-tense, creating a gap that forces leadership teams to either manufacture false progress narratives or admit their operating model isn't ready for the technology they're deploying.

THE SIGNAL

This week: the gap between AI as narrative and AI as operating result is widening, and it is starting to reprice strategies, workforces, and moats.

  • Strategy: New Fed research on 490,000 earnings calls finds that 95% of AI productivity claims are future tense, while aggregate productivity shows minimal actual gains. Strategy is what your P&L confirms, not what your earnings call promises. Read →

THE APERTURA

The AI productivity your peers describe on earnings calls is not the AI productivity their businesses are running. New Fed research covering 490,000 earnings calls found that 95% of AI productivity claims are future tense, even as aggregate productivity shows minimal actual gains. Separately, 95% of firms have deployed AI, and only one in five reports significant value. The narrative is running well ahead of the number. Boards are pricing the narrative. Owners are being asked to fund the narrative. Narrative is not strategy.

THE INSIGHT

Your board wants a story about AI. You are giving them one. That is the problem.

The board asks a version of the same question every quarter. What are we doing with AI. What is our exposure. What is the return. And every quarter you assemble the deck: the pilots underway, the vendors in evaluation, the use cases mapped, the head of AI hired. The deck is not dishonest. It is complete on its own terms. It is also, quietly, the wrong artifact.

The real problem is not that the board is asking. It is that the question forces you to report deployment activity instead of operational outcomes. So you do. And the reporting habit hardens into a strategy: keep the story ahead of the number.

The number is not moving. The Fed's read of 490,000 earnings calls confirms what most operational data already suggests. 95% of AI productivity claims across US public companies are future tense. Ninety-five percent of firms have deployed AI. Only one in five sees significant value. The gap is not a lag. The gap is a strategy problem.

The conventional response is to accelerate. More pilots, more agents, more platform spend. That response mistakes narrative urgency for operational urgency. The narrative urgency is real. The board feels it. The market rewards it. But treating a narrative signal as an operational signal is how technology gets deployed on top of processes that were never redesigned. The tool inherits the old workflow. The old workflow was the constraint. Nothing changes.

Real urgency belongs upstream. Before you scale AI, you have to name one thing. What operating metric the deployment is supposed to move, by how much, and by when. If you cannot state that, you are not deploying a strategy. You are deploying a story. The moment the board can measure the number instead of hear the story, the story stops working.

In most mid-market firms, the leadership team has never been asked whether a deployment is moving the metric before the deployment scales. The team is rewarded for shipping tools, not for pausing them. You have to change what gets rewarded before you can change what gets built. That reset only takes when the owner brings the number into the room the board is already sitting in.

This week. Bring one number to your next board meeting. Output per person, cycle time on your top three processes, or gross margin per employee, measured before AI deployment and now. If the delta is zero, pause the next deployment and put the process on the table instead. The number is the only artifact the board cannot rewrite into a story.

THE FIELD REPORT

Caterpillar's stock lost nearly a quarter of its value last month heading into earnings. Investors were reassessing whether the enthusiasm behind treating it as an AI play had run too far ahead of the underlying business, according to Bloomberg.

Our view is that the Caterpillar selloff is an early tell. Every operator whose valuation, board conversation, or capital access has been pulled forward by an AI narrative should be watching this. When a stock loses a quarter of its value, the market has decided the AI thesis moved faster than the numbers. That is the market doing what most boards have not yet done. It is repricing the story against the P&L. For US operators in the $5M to $100M range, this arrives one step later, and quieter. Lenders will start asking what changed operationally. Buyers will start asking to see the metric, not the deck. What this signals is that the window for narrative productivity is closing. The operators who redesigned processes before scaling technology will have a number to show when it does.
