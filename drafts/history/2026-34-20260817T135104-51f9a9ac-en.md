## THE INSIGHT

Your AI agents are making decisions you have not authorized. Not because they broke a rule. Because you never wrote one.

The scene is now common in mid-sized firms. An agent triages your inbox. Another drafts vendor responses. A third handles a supply chain exception before it reaches your operations lead. You deployed them for speed, and they delivered speed. What they also did was quietly absorb a category of decisions that used to route through a human. You now have no record of which decisions those were.

The instinct is to call this a governance problem and buy monitoring software. It is not a monitoring problem. It is an articulation problem. In most mid-market firms, decision rights were never written down for the humans either. Escalation was rational because ambiguity was the default. Humans handled that ambiguity by asking. Agents handle it by acting.

This is the point most owners miss. When you install an agent on top of an undefined operating model, the agent does not surface the ambiguity. It consumes it. Every judgment call the agent quietly resolves is a judgment call you can no longer see. What used to be a visible bottleneck at the owner's desk is now an invisible substitution at the machine's level.

The people cost is direct. This happens because the managers who were about to develop the judgment they needed to grow into their roles now watch an agent make the call instead. The learning loop closes before it opens. Trust in the operating model erodes, not because the agent is wrong, but because nobody can tell what it is deciding.

Operating Model OS requires one artifact before automation begins. A decision-routing map. Three tiers, and this version is different from the human version. Tier one: decisions the agent handles fully. The rule is written, the boundary is bounded, the outcome is logged. Tier two: decisions the agent drafts, but a named human approves before action. Tier three: decisions the agent surfaces to a human immediately, with context, and does not act on until a person decides.

The discipline sits in tier three. This is the list of calls where owner judgment must stay visible. Pricing exceptions. Client escalations above a threshold. People decisions. Anything that shapes reputation or precedent. Write the tiers, then embed them in the job descriptions of the humans who supervise the agents. Attach metrics that track whether tier three items are being surfaced correctly. Without those two moves, the operating model has silently transferred authority you never authorized.

This week, pull the last twenty actions your agents took. For each one, ask which tier it actually belongs to. Where a tier-three decision was handled at tier one, write the rule that pulls it back. Do it before Friday, before the pattern hardens into precedent.
